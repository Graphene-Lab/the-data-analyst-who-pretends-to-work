# 2. A Short History: From Tally Men to Data Analysts

The data analyst is not a new job. It is one of the oldest jobs there is, wearing a
very new costume. Let's walk through time and meet the ancestors of everyone who
today opens a dashboard before their coffee.

## The first analysts: the tally men

Around 8,000 years ago, in the land of Sumer (modern Iraq), people used small clay
tokens to count things: one token for one sheep, another for a jar of grain. To
"run a report" you pressed the tokens into a clay ball and wrote on the outside.
That clay ball was the world's first spreadsheet, and the person who made it was
the world's first data analyst.

The ancient Egyptians did the same on a grander scale. Every year they sent scribes
to measure the Nile's flood, count the cattle, and estimate the harvest. Why? So
the pharaoh could plan taxes and fill the granaries before a bad year. Sound
familiar? It is the same reason a modern company forecasts sales: *look at the
numbers now so you are not surprised later.*

## Counting people: the census

Almost every empire kept a census — a headcount of its people, for taxes and for
armies. The Romans took a census every few years (the word *census* is simply Latin
for "counting"). Han China counted its population centuries before Europe caught
up. These were not idle curiosities. A ruler who did not know how many people he
had could not tax them, feed them, or defend them.

## 1662: a man finds a pattern in death records

In London, plague deaths were published every week in sheets called the *Bills of
Mortality*. Most people read them and felt sad. One man, a haberdasher named **John
Graunt**, read them and saw a *pattern*. He counted, grouped, and compared years of
death records and published a small book in 1662. He noticed that deaths rose and
fell in predictable ways, that more boys were born than girls, and that the city
could be understood as a system, not a chaos.

Historians call Graunt the father of demography and of statistics. He did it with
nothing but a pile of public records and a curious mind. That is the whole method,
right there, 360 years ago.

## 1801: a Scotsman makes numbers visible

A Scottish engineer named **William Playfair** was frustrated. Tables of numbers
bored people, and bored people made no decisions. So he invented three things we
now take for granted: the **line chart**, the **bar chart**, and the **pie chart**.
Playfair turned numbers into pictures, and pictures into understanding. Every chart
you have ever seen descends from him.

## 1858: the chart that saved thousands of lives

This is the most important story in the history of data, and most people have never
heard it.

In the Crimean War, **Florence Nightingale** ran a military hospital at Scutari.
During her first winter there, **4,077 British soldiers died** — and **ten times
more died from disease than from battle wounds**. The hospital was filthy: bad
sewers, no ventilation, overcrowding.

Nightingale was also a sharp statistician. She drew a chart — she called it a
"coxcomb", a set of colored wedges — showing that almost all deaths came from
preventable infection, not from enemy fire. She put it in front of Members of
Parliament who would never have read a table of numbers but could not look away
from the picture.

The result: a Sanitary Commission was sent, the sewers were flushed, the wards were
ventilated, and the death rate fell from around **42% to 2%**. A chart changed a
policy and saved thousands of lives. That is the power of what you do when you
analyse data well. In 1859 Nightingale became the **first woman elected to the Royal
Statistical Society** — not for nursing, but for statistics.

## 1890: the machine that became IBM

Counting a whole country by hand was painfully slow. The 1880 U.S. census took
**eight years** to process — the data was stale before it was finished. A young
statistician named **Herman Hollerith** invented a machine that read **punched
cards**: a hole in a position meant "yes", no hole meant "no". Electrical contacts
counted the holes automatically.

The 1890 census, with more people and more questions than ever, finished in about
**six years — two years faster** than the smaller one before it. Hollerith started a
company to sell his machines. In 1911 it merged into a larger firm, and in 1924 that
firm was renamed **International Business Machines — IBM**. The punched card ruled
data processing for nearly a century.

## 1979: the spreadsheet comes home

For a long time, only big institutions could analyse data. Then came the personal
computer, and with it **VisiCalc** (1979) — the first spreadsheet. Suddenly an
ordinary person could put numbers in boxes and see them add up, sort, and update.
The power to analyse moved from the institution to the individual. Excel, born in
1985, carried that torch for decades.

## 1989: someone gives the job a name

In 1989 an analyst named **Howard Dresner** coined the term **"business
intelligence"** — using data to make better business decisions. Tools appeared to
pull company data into dashboards that updated themselves. The analyst's job got a
title, a toolkit, and a seat at the table.

## 2010s: everyone gets a dashboard

Tools like **Power BI** made powerful analysis cheap and visual. A small business
could now have the kind of live dashboards that once cost a fortune. The barrier
fell again.

## Now: the assistant that does it for you

And here we are. The latest step is not a faster chart or a bigger spreadsheet. It
is an **assistant** — an AI that listens to a plain-English question and does the
whole chain of work: finds the data, writes the query, builds the measure, checks
the answer. That is what this book is about, and it is the natural end of a very
long line that starts with a clay token and a curious Sumerian.

Every ancestor above had the same goal you have: turn raw numbers into a decision.
The tools changed. The job — and the curiosity behind it — never did.

## What you'll carry from this chapter

- Data analysis is ancient: counting the harvest *is* data analysis.
- Charts (Playfair) and honest statistics (Graunt) turned numbers into insight.
- A good chart can literally save lives (Nightingale).
- The tools keep getting faster and cheaper — now an AI does the chain for you.
